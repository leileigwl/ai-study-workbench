### Q1.1 Why are rent-controlled apartments much harder to find two years later?
- **Key Points (`3`)**:
  1. **Quantity Demanded Rises**: Cheaper rent pulls more people into the market (fewer roommates, staying longer, newcomers moving in).
  2. **Quantity Supplied Falls**: Landlords stop building new rentals, convert apartments to offices/condos, or stop fixing old buildings.
  3. **Non-Price Rationing Replaces Price**: Because money can't clear the gap, apartments get allocated via waitlists, connections, key-money bribes, or discrimination.
- **Follow-Up Prompts if Missed**:
  - *If missing Point 2*: `"Renters want cheaper apartments, sure—but put yourself in a builder's shoes deciding whether to construct 200 new apartments or build luxury offices instead. What happens to new housing supply over 2 years?"`
  - *If missing Point 3*: `"Suppose 1,000 people line up for 100 cheap apartments. If the landlord legally cannot pick whoever offers \$1,500, how do they end up picking who gets the keys?"`

### Q1.2 Concert ticket prices jump overnight after a superstar wins an award—did Supply shift or Demand shift?
- **Key Points (`2`)**:
  1. **Demand Curve Shifted Right**: Willingness to pay increased at every price because of external buzz/preference shock.
  2. **Supply Quantity Stays Fixed (Stadium Seats)**: Higher ticket prices reflect movement along a steep/vertical supply capacity, not supply shifting left.
- **Follow-Up Prompts if Missed**:
  - *If confusing Supply vs Demand*: `"Did the stadium lose half its seats overnight, or did twice as many fans suddenly want tickets at the old \$100 price?"`
